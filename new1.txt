\begin{abstract}
Wireless sensor networks deployed in power distribution facilities must deliver mixed-criticality traffic across battery-limited links, balancing urgent trip alerts with routine monitoring flows. This paper investigates deep reinforcement learning for queue scheduling through an integrated ns-3 and OpenAI Gym framework. We formulate a Double Deep Q-Network scheduler incorporating prioritized replay and state-dependent reward guidance to prevent queue starvation. Over 20 matched, seed-controlled simulation trials against Strict Priority and random baselines, the learned controller improves upon random allocation and delivers 95.78\% of high-priority packets. Although it does not match the high-priority latency of the Strict Priority heuristic, the agent reduces normal telemetry delay by 30.5\% by dynamically balancing buffer occupancy. We document these findings as a reproducible benchmark clarifying the operational trade-offs of learning-based schedulers in smart utility communications.

\keywords{Smart Energy Systems \and Wireless Sensor Networks \and Reinforcement Learning \and ns-3 \and QoS \and Priority Scheduling}
\end{abstract}

\section{Introduction}
Modern power grids depend on wireless sensor networks (WSNs) to monitor substations, track distribution feeders, and signal transient electrical faults. Nodes in these setups operate with limited energy reserves and face stochastic channel conditions. Their traffic splits into two distinct classes: critical alarm bursts (breaker trips and protection triggers) and routine continuous telemetry (load levels and voltage readings). Grid protection mandates that fault signals arrive with low latency, while steady state estimation requires that routine telemetry avoid buffer exhaustion \cite{frikha2021wrlsurvey,leong2020automatica}.

When traffic surges congest local queues, gateway scheduling dictates the overall Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR), deadline compliance, buffer drops, and node lifetime. Conventional deployments rely on Strict Priority (SP) because it is simple, deterministic, and requires minimal compute. Under stationary workloads, Strict Priority forwards high-priority (HP) traffic ahead of normal-priority (NP) traffic. However, during extended grid disturbance events when protection alarms fire repeatedly, Strict Priority starves normal telemetry queues. This causes buffer overflow and delayed state-estimation updates at the operations center.

Deep Reinforcement Learning (DRL) provides an adaptive alternative by casting scheduling as a Markov Decision Process (MDP), allowing edge gateways to learn dynamic queuing strategies from environmental feedback \cite{luong2019drlsurvey}. Yet training model-free agents in communication systems introduces instability, sample inefficiency, and potential policy collapse. Unconstrained reward functions often bias agents toward degenerate behaviors, such as serving only normal traffic to collect frequent, safe rewards while dropping critical alarms.

We examine a policy-guided Double-DQN packet scheduler through a co-simulation environment linking PyTorch and the ns-3 network simulator via ns3-gym \cite{ns3gym,boni2021ns3gym}. We evaluate whether a learned DRL policy can safeguard critical traffic while remaining competitive with Strict Priority across repeatable, seed-synchronized trials.

Our primary contributions include:
\begin{itemize}
    \item An open-source co-simulation testbed connecting ns-3.40 and PyTorch, instrumented for explicit per-class packet tracking and run-level artifact verification.
    \item A soft policy-guidance reward and validation score that tracks HP generation, delivery, and age, preventing policy collapse without forcing static rule mimicry.
    \item A statistical evaluation across 20 matched, seed-synchronized simulation runs comparing the learned controller against Strict Priority and random baselines.
    \item An empirical trade-off analysis showing that while Strict Priority yields lower HP latency, the learned policy reduces normal telemetry delay by 30.5\% while sustaining 95.78\% HP packet delivery.
\end{itemize}

\section{Related Work}
Surveys document how reinforcement learning addresses medium access control, dynamic routing, and packet scheduling \cite{luong2019drlsurvey,frikha2021wrlsurvey}. These reviews point out practical deployment hurdles, notably sample inefficiency, absent safety bounds, and poor generalization under shifting traffic regimes.

In WSN research, DRL handles stochastic channel access and energy management. Leong et al. showed that model-free DRL handles uncertain channel fades during sensor transmissions \cite{leong2020automatica}. Sharma and Chauhan implemented distributed RL to sustain sensor coverage while cutting power drain \cite{sharma2020wsn}. Dutta and Biswas applied multi-agent RL to mitigate medium access contention in dense IoT topologies \cite{dutta2022comnet}, while Barker and Swany utilized cooperative RL to discover forwarding paths in multi-hop networks \cite{barker2022wcnc}.

Other efforts focus on hardware constraints. Mishra and Liang designed an RL sleep scheduler that extends node lifetime via low-power states \cite{mishra2023sleep}. Nagib et al. evaluated safety and acceleration strategies for wireless DRL, focusing on reward shaping to constrain exploration \cite{nagib2023safe}. Kim et al. verified adaptive DRL schedulers meeting real-time latency deadlines in wireless time-sensitive systems \cite{kim2024wtsn}.

We focus on systems-level verification within smart grid sensing. We provide run-level artifacts, explicit HP tracking, and validation-gated policy selection to define the practical capabilities of learned schedulers compared to standard heuristics.

\section{System Model and Problem Formulation}

\subsection{Network and Traffic Model}
We model an edge sensing cluster representative of a smart grid distribution substation. The network comprises 100 wireless sensor nodes distributed uniformly at random across a 150\,m $\times$ 150\,m planar area, communicating with a central sink gateway located at the center (75\,m, 75\,m). The physical and MAC layers are implemented in ns-3.40 using the IEEE 802.15.4 standard operating in the 2.4\,GHz ISM band with an unslotted CSMA/CA access mechanism, a raw data rate of 250\,kbps, transmit power of 0\,dBm, and a log-distance path loss model ($\gamma = 3.0$). Initial energy per node is configured at 10.0\,J.

Sensor nodes generate two distinct traffic classes: High-Priority (HP) packets representing critical grid disturbance alarms, fault indications, and protection triggers, and Normal-Priority (NP) packets representing periodic telemetry, power-quality measurements, and environmental status updates. High-priority traffic is generated according to an unannounced, bursty Poisson process with arrival intensity $\lambda_H = 0.2$\,packets/s per active node and a payload size of 50 bytes. Normal telemetry traffic follows regular periodic generation with interval $T_N = 2.0$\,s and a payload size of 100 bytes. Each sensor maintains two dedicated, finite-capacity FIFO buffers: an HP buffer of capacity $B_H = 50$ packets and an NP buffer of capacity $B_N = 100$ packets. Packets remaining in either buffer beyond a deadline threshold ($T_H^{\text{max}} = 1.0$\,s, $T_N^{\text{max}} = 3.0$\,s) are dropped as expired timeouts. The scheduling agent situated at the gateway operates at a discrete decision interval of $\Delta t = 100$\,ms over finite simulation episodes spanning 5000 decision steps (500 seconds of simulated time).

\subsection{MDP Definition}
We formulate queue scheduling as a discounted Markov Decision Process (MDP) with finite simulation episodes, defined by the tuple $(\mathcal{S}, \mathcal{A}, \mathcal{P}, \mathcal{R}, \gamma)$. At interval $t$, the gateway agent observes state $\mathbf{s}_t \in \mathcal{S}$ and executes action $a_t \in \mathcal{A}$.

The state space contains 6 normalized metrics:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{s}_t = \left[ q_t^{N},\; q_t^{H},\; e_t,\; a_t^{N},\; a_t^{H},\; d_t \right],
\end{equation}
where $q_t^{N}, q_t^{H} \in [0, 1]$ represent queue occupancy ratios; $e_t \in [0, 1]$ reflects residual energy; $a_t^{N}, a_t^{H} \in [0, 1]$ denote head-of-line packet ages normalized to expiration deadlines; and $d_t \in [0, 1]$ measures active neighbor density.

The action set comprises 5 discrete commands:
\begin{equation}
\mathcal{A} = \{\text{send HP (0)},\; \text{send normal (1)},\; \text{drop normal (2)},\; \text{sleep (3)},\; \text{drop HP (4)}\}.
\end{equation}
Actions 0 and 1 transmit packets from the respective buffers. Action 3 puts the radio into sleep mode for 100\,ms to save power. Actions 2 and 4 drop stale packets during congestion to prevent buffer bloat.

\subsection{Reward Formulation with Soft Policy Guidance}
Mixed-criticality scheduling requires balanced reward design. Rewarding raw throughput alone leads to policy collapse, as normal packets dominate arrival rates and the agent learns to ignore critical alerts. Conversely, excessive penalties for any HP delay force the network to imitate Strict Priority, eliminating operational flexibility.

We address this with a bounded reward function using hyperbolic tangent scaling:
\begin{equation}
r_t = \tanh\left(\frac{r_t^{\text{base}} + r_t^{\text{guide}}}{100}\right).
\end{equation}
The base component reflects primary networking performance:
\begin{equation}
r_t^{\text{base}} = w_1 N_{\text{sent}}^H + w_2 N_{\text{sent}}^N + w_3 N_{\text{drop}}^H + w_4 N_{\text{drop}}^N + w_5 N_{\text{to}}^H + w_6 N_{\text{to}}^N + w_7 (a_t^H)^2 + w_8 (a_t^N)^2 - w_9 \Delta e_t,
\end{equation}
where $N_{\text{sent}}^H$ and $N_{\text{sent}}^N$ represent confirmed deliveries ($w_1 = 25.0, w_2 = 5.0$); $N_{\text{drop}}$ and $N_{\text{to}}$ penalize buffer overflow drops and timeout expirations ($w_3 = -40.0, w_4 = -8.0, w_5 = -80.0, w_6 = -10.0$); quadratic terms penalize waiting queue latencies ($w_7 = -40.0, w_8 = -2.0$); and $\Delta e_t$ penalizes energy depletion ($w_9 = 250.0$). If the scheduler selects sleep (Action 3), it receives a conservation bonus $r_{\text{sleep}} = +0.5$.

To ensure the agent reliably services critical traffic without eliminating operational flexibility, we introduce the soft policy guidance term:
\begin{equation}
r_t^{\text{guide}} =
\begin{cases}
+\alpha, & a_t = 0 \text{ and } q_t^H > 0\\
-\beta, & a_t \neq 0,\; q_t^H > 0,\; a_t^H > \tau_u\\
0, & \text{otherwise}
\end{cases}
\end{equation}
with bonus $\alpha = 6.0$, penalty magnitude $\beta = 8.0$, and urgency age threshold $\tau_u = 0.55$. Under this formulation, the agent receives positive reinforcement for servicing HP packets. Critically, the omission penalty $-\beta$ is applied only when the waiting HP packet is urgent ($a_t^H > \tau_u$). This design grants the scheduler autonomy to temporarily withhold a newly arrived HP packet to flush an aging normal queue, intervening with negative feedback only when an HP packet approaches critical delay boundaries.

\section{Methodology}

\subsection{Learning Architecture}
We adopt a Double Deep Q-Network (Double-DQN) to reduce positive maximization bias during Q-learning updates. In this setup, online parameters $\theta_t$ select the greedy action, while target parameters $\theta_t^{-}$ estimate its value:
\begin{equation}
Y_t^{\text{DoubleQ}} = r_{t+1} + \gamma Q\left(\mathbf{s}_{t+1}, \operatorname{arg\,max}_{a} Q(\mathbf{s}_{t+1}, a; \theta_t);\, \theta_t^{-}\right).
\end{equation}

Transitions are stored in a Prioritized Experience Replay (PER) buffer of 100,000 samples. Transitions are sampled with probability $P(i) = p_i^\mu / \sum_k p_k^\mu$, using temporal difference error $p_i = |\delta_i| + \epsilon_{\text{per}}$ with prioritization parameter $\mu = 0.6$. Importance sampling weights $w_i = (N \cdot P(i))^{-\nu}$ correct for distribution distortion, annealing $\nu$ from $0.4$ to $1.0$ over 100,000 steps.

The neural architecture employs an MLP with two hidden layers of 64 ReLU neurons mapping $|\mathcal{S}|=6$ inputs to $|\mathcal{A}|=5$ outputs. Target network weights update via Polyak averaging:
\begin{equation}
\theta^{-} \leftarrow \tau \theta + (1 - \tau)\theta^{-},
\end{equation}
with $\tau = 0.005$. Optimization uses Adam with learning rate $1 \times 10^{-4}$ decayed via Cosine Annealing over 50 epochs, with gradient clipping at $L_2$ norm 1.0. Exploration follows an $\epsilon$-greedy schedule decaying from $1.0$ to $0.05$ (decay factor $0.99995$) after 4000 warm-up steps. Evaluation sets $\epsilon = 0.0$ for deterministic execution.

\subsection{Baselines and Validation Protocol}
We benchmark the proposed policy-guided DQN against two baseline scheduling schemes:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Strict Priority (SP):} Transmits HP packets whenever $q_t^H > 0$, otherwise transmits normal packets if $q_t^N > 0$, or sleeps when idle.
    \item \textbf{Random:} Uniformly samples actions at random from $\mathcal{A}$ at each decision step, representing the performance floor.
\end{itemize}

Training runs for 50 epochs of 5000 steps each. Checkpoints are evaluated after every epoch across five fixed validation seeds ($99991$--$99995$) using a multi-metric score:
\begin{equation}
S_{\text{val}} = 0.40 \left(\frac{\text{PDR}}{100}\right) + 0.10 \left(1 - \frac{\text{PLR}}{100}\right) + 0.35 \left(\frac{\text{HR}}{100}\right) + 0.15 \left(1 - \min\left(\frac{D_H}{1.0}, 1.0\right)\right),
\end{equation}
where $\text{HR}$ is the HP delivery percentage and $D_H$ is mean HP delay in seconds. If HP packets are generated but none delivered ($\text{HR} = 0$), a penalty of $-0.25$ is subtracted. Early stopping halts training after 15 stagnated epochs.

The final evaluation tests 20 matched runs per policy across identical seeds ($12545$--$12564$). Table~\ref{tab:setup} provides hyperparameter details.

\subsection{Statistical Evaluation Protocol}
Evaluated metrics include aggregate throughput (kbps), Packet Delivery Ratio (PDR \%), Packet Loss Ratio (PLR \%), average normal packet delay (s), average HP packet delay (s), specific energy consumption per delivered bit (nJ/bit), and the HP delivery ratio (\%). All metrics are reported as the sample mean alongside their 95\% confidence intervals (CI) computed using Student's $t$-distribution.

For pairwise significance testing between the proposed DQN scheduler and Strict Priority across the 20 matched trials, we verify the normality of paired differences via the Shapiro-Wilk test at $\alpha = 0.05$. Paired differences for throughput ($p = 0.412$), PDR ($p = 0.385$), PLR ($p = 0.298$), HP delay ($p = 0.514$), energy ($p = 0.621$), and HP delivery ratio ($p = 0.187$) conformed to normal distributions, for which we report two-tailed paired Student's $t$-tests. Normal-delay paired differences departed from normality ($p = 0.0032 < 0.05$), for which we employ the non-parametric Wilcoxon signed-rank test. Practical effect sizes are characterized using paired Cohen's $d_z = \bar{D} / \sigma_D$, where $\bar{D}$ and $\sigma_D$ represent the sample mean and standard deviation of the paired differences across the 20 matched runs.

\subsection{Comparative Analysis Against Heuristic Baselines}
Compared with the Random baseline, the learned scheduler improves throughput, PDR, and HP delivery while reducing packet loss, normal-packet delay, HP-packet delay, and energy consumed per delivered bit. Specifically, throughput rises from 2.8000\,kbps to 7.0267\,kbps, PDR increases from 20.07\% to 50.33\%, average HP latency decreases from 0.3521\,s to 0.1431\,s, and energy per delivered bit drops from 806.70\,nJ/bit to 443.87\,nJ/bit. Furthermore, the HP delivery ratio rises from 34.49\% under random selection to 95.78\% under the learned controller.

When compared against the dedicated Strict Priority baseline, the learned agent does not achieve superior throughput or HP delay. Strict Priority records an average throughput of 7.9104\,kbps, an overall PDR of 56.66\%, an average HP latency of 0.0274\,s, and an HP delivery ratio of 98.34\%. The paired statistical tests in Table~\ref{tab:stats} confirm that these differences are statistically significant ($p < 10^{-11}$), with large effect sizes reflecting the deterministic behavior of the benchmark.

\subsection{Trade-Off Analysis in Smart Grid Telemetry}
The quantitative divergence between DQN-Edge and Strict Priority demonstrates a practical scheduling compromise. Strict Priority behaves as a greedy heuristic; it prioritizes immediate HP transmissions regardless of normal queue status. In smart utility applications characterized by bursty fault activity, this behavior induces buffer overflow for routine measurements, depriving grid operators of continuous current, voltage, and thermal sensor readings.

The policy-guided DQN scheduler learns a cooperative balancing policy. By observing head-of-line packet ages ($a_t^H, a_t^N$), the agent learns that newly arrived HP packets can tolerate brief queuing periods without violating their 1.0\,s deadline. During these brief windows, the agent opportunistically clears pending normal telemetry packets before they expire. By accepting a 2.56\% reduction in HP delivery ratio and a 115.7\,ms increase in HP delay, the learned scheduler delivers a 30.5\% improvement in normal packet latency, preserving telemetry flow while maintaining 95.78\% delivery for critical protection alerts.

\subsection{Diagnostic Analysis: Reward Shaping vs. Policy Collapse}
Our development process provided an important lesson regarding reward formulation in communications DRL. In earlier experiments using an unconstrained reward function (V74 configuration), the scheduler suffered from complete policy collapse: the agent converged to a degenerate policy that selected Action 1 (send normal) in 100\% of decision steps. Because normal packets were abundant, transmitting normal traffic provided steady rewards, whereas attempting HP transmissions incurred risk due to bursty arrivals and contention. In that run, zero HP packets were delivered. However, because the simulator contained a zero-division safeguard defaulting average delay to 0.0 when no packets were delivered, the output superficially reported ``0.0000\,s delay''---a deceptive artifact that masked complete high-priority starvation.

Introducing the soft policy guidance in V76, paired with an HP-aware validation scoring metric, resolved this pathology. By penalizing the scheduler only when an HP packet aged past $\tau_u = 0.55$, the agent was steered away from trivial local optima without sacrificing its ability to dynamically manage queues. This highlights that reinforcement learning applied to communication protocols requires explicit per-class packet tracking, action distribution validation, and tail latency profiling rather than aggregate reward monitoring alone.

\section{Discussion}
Our findings show that the Double-DQN policy learns a stable queue-service strategy that balances HP and NP traffic more effectively than the Random baseline. However, Strict Priority's lower HP delay underscores structural limits in unconstrained DRL models. First, the 6-dimensional observation vector provides partial observability, omitting physical-layer channel state information. Second, single-agent Q-learning struggles to match deterministic heuristics on boundary cases without explicit safety overrides.

Future research should explore three areas:
\begin{itemize}
    \item \textbf{Constrained and Safe RL:} Formulating the task as a Constrained MDP via Lagrangian relaxation or action projection layers to enforce hard bounds on HP delay.
    \item \textbf{Distributional Value Approximation:} Utilizing Distributional RL (such as QR-DQN or IQN) to capture queuing delay distributions and mitigate tail-latency outliers.
    \item \textbf{Out-of-Distribution Testing:} Evaluating trained policies under varying node counts, traffic ratios, and interference profiles to assess model transferability.
\end{itemize}

\section{Threats to Validity}
Several limitations apply to our findings. First, all trials were run inside ns-3.40. While ns-3 provides a detailed simulation model of IEEE 802.15.4 CSMA/CA, hardware deployments introduce clock drift, transceiver switching jitter, and multipath effects that are not fully represented in our simulation.

Second, the observed boundaries reflect our specific workload (100 nodes, 100\,ms control intervals, fixed packet timeouts). Altering high-priority traffic ratios or node densities could shift the trade-off surface between Strict Priority and DRL. Finally, our setup focuses on single-hop cluster-head scheduling; extending these controllers to multi-hop meshed forwarding topologies represents an open research challenge.

\section{Conclusion}
This study presented a reproducible, statistically grounded evaluation of deep reinforcement learning for priority-aware packet scheduling in wireless sensor networks supporting smart energy infrastructure. By coupling ns-3 with PyTorch via ns3-gym, we trained a policy-guided Double-DQN agent over mixed-criticality traffic workloads. Across 20 seed-synchronized evaluation runs, the learned scheduler outperformed random baseline allocation and preserved a 95.78\% high-priority delivery ratio, though it did not match Strict Priority in high-priority latency or throughput. However, the learned policy demonstrated balanced queue service, achieving a 30.5\% reduction in normal telemetry delay and mitigating buffer starvation.
